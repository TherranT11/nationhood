Nationhood — Player Guide
A multiplayer political simulation. Pick a nation, pick a role, build a career in a world that runs on its own clock.
ALPHA · CRUCERA SHARD
What is Nationhood?

Nationhood is a persistent, multiplayer political simulation. You pick a real-world-inspired nation on a shared map and step into it as a single person — a politician, an entrepreneur, a military officer — not as a faceless government. Every other person in that nation is either another human player or an AI filling the room, and they're all making their own moves at the same time you are.

The world runs on a shared clock. A “tick” advances the simulation a fixed amount of in-game time, on a schedule the server keeps. Bills move through committee, elections resolve, courts hand down verdicts, corporations earn revenue, and your opponents take their actions whether you're logged in or not. Logging in is how you steer your character through what the world is already doing; it is not the thing that makes the world happen.

You don't “win” Nationhood. There is no end screen. What you build is a career — a record of offices held, cases tried, businesses founded, alliances made, scandals survived. That record persists across years of in-game time, and the choices you make on a quiet Tuesday afternoon ripple outward into races, verdicts, and markets weeks later.

Roles you can play

You can hold several characters simultaneously across the same shard:

  • Politician — climb the career ladder from grassroots organising to Parliament, the judiciary, or the civil service. Up to four politician slots per account.
  • Entrepreneur — found and run corporations across industries from banking to aviation.
  • Military Officer — command an army faction inside a nation. (Currently outside the politician build path.)

Each role has its own dashboard, its own stat sheet, and its own ways of accruing power. They share a world, though — an entrepreneur's bank sues a rival corp in the same courts your Magistrate politician sits on; a Parliament-passed tax policy lands on the entrepreneur's revenue the same tick it hits everyone else's.

Getting Started — pick your direction

Three faction types — three different games sitting on top of the same world. You're not locked in: switching between factions you already own is free, and most veteran players end up running two or three at once (a politician, an entrepreneur, maybe an army). But for your first character, lean into whichever of these descriptions sounds like the kind of session you want to log into.

The Politician — if you like long games and political intrigue

What a session looks like. Pick one repeatable action from your role card (door-knock a precinct, file a memo, give a speech), watch the dice resolve, then probably close the tab until the next tick. Big moves — running for office, sitting the bar exam, taking the bench — happen rarely and matter a lot. The career stretches across in-game years.

Pace. Slow and episodic. A single race resolves on one tick. You spend most ticks just positioning for the move you'll make six ticks from now.

You'll like this if you like: RPG-style progression where stats and decisions accumulate; following a single named character through a multi-year arc; reading the room and timing things; the negotiation side of politics (parties, alliances, factions inside factions).

You'll bounce off if: you want immediate, dense decision-making each session. The politician is patient by design.

The Entrepreneur — if you like building businesses

What a session looks like. Check your corp's books. Respond to contract RFPs, bid on construction work, accept or refuse loan applications, file your taxes, evaluate whether to take the company public. There's almost always something operational to do, because the corp doesn't stop running between your visits.

Pace. Opportunistic and continuous. You react to whatever the market and the policy environment threw at you since you last logged in.

You'll like this if you like: tycoon games and business sims; spreadsheet-shaped decisions; building something complex from a small seed; competing with other players across the same industry; reading economic data and trying to outguess the policy direction.

You'll bounce off if: you don't enjoy economic systems or you want a single dramatic decision per session instead of a steady stream of small ones.

The Military Officer — if you like command and wargames

What a session looks like. Manage your branch within a nation's armed forces — the Army, the Air Force, etc. — advance through the rank ladder (Platoon Leader to Chief of Army Staff; Pilot Officer to Chief of Air Staff), assign units, prepare for the conflicts your nation is sliding into. Much quieter in peacetime; intense when the politicians can't keep the peace.

Pace. Punctuated. Long stretches of garrison work and career advancement, then sharp bursts of activity when something breaks.

You'll like this if you like: wargames, hierarchical command structures, military fiction, the cold logic of force posture and deterrence.

You'll bounce off if: you want continuous, dense decision-making each session; the military is feast-or-famine by design.

If you're still not sure

Start as a politician. It has the most-developed onboarding (the Tier-1 Community Organizer surface is the most-polished part of the build), the lowest setup friction (no founding capital to commit, no branch to pick), and the broadest connection to the rest of the world — politicians vote on laws that hit entrepreneurs, sit on the courts that arbitrate corp disputes, and run the ministries military officers report up to. Even if you eventually find your home in another role, a politician character is the cheapest way to see how the rest of the game fits together.

You can found an entrepreneur or join the military later from the same account — up to four politicians plus one of each other role type, all living on the same shard at the same time.

The Politician path

The politician role is about becoming someone in your nation — not running a party from above, but rising through one as an individual. You start as a young Party Member with nothing but a name and a free first action, and the shape of your career depends on which ladder you decide to climb.

Stats

A politician is described by four numbers, each surfaced on the role card and the topbar pill:

  • Reputation — how the public and your peers perceive you. Slow to gain, slow to lose. Gates appointments, judicial readiness, and senior office.
  • Skill — raw competence. Modifies dice rolls on actions, speeches, fundraising, election matchups.
  • Influence — your network reach. Earned through chamber time, MP race wins, speeches, sponsoring resolutions. Spent on lobbying, vote-whipping, calling in favours, pushing appointments.
  • Capital — money and favours banked. Liquid, transactional. Earned via salaries, party funds, kickbacks, memos, race payouts. Spent on race entry fees, party donations, action costs.

The three career paths

From Party Member onward the ladder forks. You don't have to commit to one fork forever — some politicians end up wearing two hats — but each path is its own progression of offices, costs, and gates.

  • Elected office. Party Member → Community Organizer (local, Tier 1) → City Council Member (Tier 2 local) or Senior MP → Member of Parliament → ministerial and party-leadership tiers. Races are won on a Skill matchup against either an auto-generated opponent or another player.
  • The judiciary. Pass the Bar exam → Advocate → Experienced Advocate → State Advocate (appointed by the Interior Minister) or Magistrate (Take the Bench). Advocates take cases on contract; magistrates preside. Bench tiers replace lower titles in the topbar pill.
  • The civil service. Sit the civil-service exam to be assigned to a ministry (Defense, Foreign Affairs & Trade, Economic Development, Interior). Civil servants file paperwork and memos for the state in exchange for steady Reputation and Capital, and serve as the institutional memory of their ministry.

How a tick plays

Every tick you pick one repeatable action from your current role's card — a Community Organizer might Door-Knock, give a Speech, or Build the Base; a Civil Servant files Paperwork or a Memo; an Advocate takes or refuses a drawn case. Each action is a dice roll modified by a stat, with bracketed outcomes printed on the card so you can plan a turn before you click.

Joining a party unlocks a second class of actions — campaign work, fundraising, lobbying ministers — and gives you a stage to compete for office on. You don't have to join one to play, but the parliamentary and ministerial rungs require it.

National politics, local politics, and your first race

Once you've joined a party, an Election Offer opens up on your Career page. It presents two races side by side, and the choice between them is the first real strategic decision you'll make as a politician.

Local politics — the Community Board. This is a one-tick race for a Community Organizer seat in your district. The opponent is an auto-generated independent, the Skill matchup is small (you vs. roughly 2–4), and the loss case is harmless — you give up a tick and walk away. The win case is your first office: a 9-tick term running grassroots work, plus a starter Capital payout and a Reputation bump. Almost every politician should start here. It exists to teach you the system without risking anything you can't recover.

National politics — Parliament. A parliamentary race plays for the national stage. The Skill matchup is bigger (a wider opponent range and a higher die), the stake on the win side is the largest single payout a low-tier politician can take, and the stake on the loss side hurts — reputation, influence, capital, all real. You also wait several ticks for the result instead of one. This is the race you take after you've banked some Skill at the local level, or that you take cold if you're feeling brave.

How a race actually resolves. Skill drives both races: your Skill stat is rolled against the opponent's, the matchup converts to a polling percentage that gets snapshotted on the active-race card, and the result lands at the resolve tick. You can spend the ticks in between running campaign actions to nudge the polling — ads, rallies, door-knocking — or sitting tight and trusting the matchup. Win and the system stamps your office, pays out the rewards, and adds the entry to your career log. Lose and the loss is logged just as visibly: a defeat sits in your career history alongside your wins, and people remember.

Recommended first arc. Join a party. Run for the Community Organizer seat. Serve the 9-tick term using the Tier-1 action card (Door Knock, Speech, Build the Base). When the term ends, decide whether you want to climb laterally to City Council Member — another local race, slightly more contested — or jump straight to a Parliament run on a strong Skill stat. By the time that decision lands, you'll have a feel for whether your home is in the local trenches or the national chamber.

The Entrepreneur path

The entrepreneur role is about building businesses inside the same political economy your politician characters are arguing over. You found a corporation, choose an industry, raise capital, sign contracts, and try to make the company outlast the policy environment.

Stats

Entrepreneurs share the same four-stat vocabulary politicians use — Reputation, Skill, Influence, Capital — but the meaning is leaned toward commerce. Reputation is how counterparties read you; Skill drives execution rolls on operations and deals; Influence opens doors with ministries and regulators; Capital is straightforwardly your money on hand.

Founding a corporation

You begin by founding a corp in one of the chartered industries:

  • Banking
  • Shipping & maritime logistics
  • Construction & engineering
  • Real estate & development
  • Civil aviation (airlines)
  • Aviation manufacturing
  • Oil & gas

Founding takes a chunk of starting capital (between $5M and $500M) plus a fixed founding fee. The corp HQs in a specific nation and lives or dies under that nation's tax code, tariff regime, and policy environment — the same one Parliament is voting on tickly. You can run the corp private (you own 100%) or take it public via an IPO to raise capital from other players, at the cost of sharing the upside.

How a tick plays

Where a politician's tick is dominated by one repeatable action, an entrepreneur's tick is dominated by the state of the corp. You manage buildings and assets, accept or reject contracts, set tax filings, and respond to RFPs from other corps and the state. Construction firms bid on infrastructure work; airlines apply for routes; banks extend credit. Each industry has its own operational surface.

Where it meets the rest of the game

Entrepreneurs aren't an island — their world is the same world the politicians are governing:

  • Politicians write the rules entrepreneurs operate under (tax brackets, tariffs, employment law, subsidies). A bad Parliament can crush a healthy corp.
  • Courts settle disputes. When two corps clash, the case goes through the judicial system the Advocate / Magistrate politicians staff.
  • Ministries award contracts. A Civil Servant inside Economic Development is the person on the other side of a corp's RFP submission.

The economy page (The Sectors of the Economy) shows every industry's strength in each nation and which corporations are operating in it — that's the room your entrepreneur is competing inside.
