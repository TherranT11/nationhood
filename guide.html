A guide to Nationhood
A multiplayer political simulation. Pick a nation, pick a role, build a career in a world that runs on its own clock.
I.What is Nationhood?

Nationhood is a persistent, multiplayer political simulation. You pick a real-world-inspired nation on a shared map and step into it as a single person — a politician, an entrepreneur, a military officer — not as a faceless government. Every other person in that nation is either another human player or an AI filling the room, and they're all making their own moves at the same time you are.

The world runs on a shared clock. A “tick” advances the simulation a fixed amount of in-game time, on a schedule the server keeps. Bills move through committee, elections resolve, courts hand down verdicts, corporations earn revenue, and your opponents take their actions whether you're logged in or not. Logging in is how you steer your character through what the world is already doing; it is not the thing that makes the world happen.

You don't “win” Nationhood. There is no end screen. What you build is a career — a record of offices held, cases tried, businesses founded, alliances made, scandals survived. That record persists across years of in-game time, and the choices you make on a quiet Tuesday afternoon ripple outward into races, verdicts, and markets weeks later.

Roles you can play

You can hold several characters simultaneously across the same shard:

  • Politician — climb the career ladder from grassroots organising to Parliament, the judiciary, or the civil service. Up to four politician slots per account.
  • Businessman — found corporations across industries from banking to aviation, or hire into another player's company and climb its ranks.
  • Military Officer — command an army faction inside a nation.

Each role has its own dashboard, its own stat sheet, and its own ways of accruing power. They share a world, though — an entrepreneur's bank sues a rival corp in the same courts your Magistrate politician sits on; a Parliament-passed tax policy lands on the entrepreneur's revenue the same tick it hits everyone else's.

II.Getting Started — pick your direction

Three faction types — three different games sitting on top of the same world. You're not locked in: switching between factions you already own is free, and most veteran players end up running two or three at once (a politician, an entrepreneur, maybe an army). But for your first character, lean into whichever of these descriptions sounds like the kind of session you want to log into.

The Politician — if you like long games and political intrigue

What a session looks like. Pick one repeatable action from your role card (door-knock a precinct, file a memo, give a speech), watch the dice resolve, then probably close the tab until the next tick. Big moves — running for office, sitting the bar exam, taking the bench — happen rarely and matter a lot. The career stretches across in-game years.

Pace. Slow and episodic. A single race resolves on one tick. You spend most ticks just positioning for the move you'll make six ticks from now.

You'll like this if you like: RPG-style progression where stats and decisions accumulate; following a single named character through a multi-year arc; reading the room and timing things; the negotiation side of politics (parties, alliances, factions inside factions).

You'll bounce off if: you want immediate, dense decision-making each session. The politician is patient by design.

The Businessman — if you like building businesses

What a session looks like. Check your corp's books. Respond to contract RFPs, bid on construction work, accept or refuse loan applications, file your taxes, evaluate whether to take the company public. There's almost always something operational to do, because the corp doesn't stop running between your visits.

Pace. Opportunistic and continuous. You react to whatever the market and the policy environment threw at you since you last logged in.

You'll like this if you like: tycoon games and business sims; spreadsheet-shaped decisions; building something complex from a small seed; competing with other players across the same industry; reading economic data and trying to outguess the policy direction.

You'll bounce off if: you don't enjoy economic systems or you want a single dramatic decision per session instead of a steady stream of small ones.

The Military Officer — if you like command and wargames

What a session looks like. Manage your branch within a nation's armed forces — the Army, the Air Force, etc. — advance through the rank ladder (Platoon Leader to Chief of Army Staff; Pilot Officer to Chief of Air Staff), assign units, prepare for the conflicts your nation is sliding into. Much quieter in peacetime; intense when the politicians can't keep the peace.

Pace. Punctuated. Long stretches of garrison work and career advancement, then sharp bursts of activity when something breaks.

You'll like this if you like: wargames, hierarchical command structures, military fiction, the cold logic of force posture and deterrence.

You'll bounce off if: you want continuous, dense decision-making each session; the military is feast-or-famine by design.

If you're still not sure

Start as a politician. It has the most-developed onboarding (the Tier-1 Community Organizer surface is the most-polished part of the build), the lowest setup friction (no founding capital to commit, no branch to pick), and the broadest connection to the rest of the world — politicians vote on laws that hit entrepreneurs, sit on the courts that arbitrate corp disputes, and run the ministries military officers report up to. Even if you eventually find your home in another role, a politician character is the cheapest way to see how the rest of the game fits together.

You can found an entrepreneur or join the military later from the same account — up to four politicians plus one of each other role type, all living on the same shard at the same time.

III.The Politician path

The politician role is about becoming someone in your nation — not running a party from above, but rising through one as an individual. You start as a young Party Member with nothing but a name and a free first action, and the shape of your career depends on which ladder you decide to climb.

Stats

A politician is described by four numbers, each surfaced on the role card and the topbar pill:

  • Reputation — how the public and your peers perceive you. Slow to gain, slow to lose. Gates appointments, judicial readiness, and senior office.
  • Experience — raw competence. Modifies dice rolls on actions, speeches, fundraising, election matchups.
  • Influence — your network reach. Earned through chamber time, MP race wins, speeches, sponsoring resolutions. Spent on lobbying, vote-whipping, calling in favours, pushing appointments.
  • Capital — money and favours banked. Liquid, transactional. Earned via salaries, party funds, kickbacks, memos, race payouts. Spent on race entry fees, party donations, action costs.

The three career paths

From Party Member onward the ladder forks. You don't have to commit to one fork forever — some politicians end up wearing two hats — but each path is its own progression of offices, costs, and gates.

  • Elected office. Party Member → Community Organizer (local, Tier 1) → City Council Member (Tier 2 local) or Senior MP → Member of Parliament → ministerial and party-leadership tiers. Races are won on an Experience matchup against either an auto-generated opponent or another player.
  • The judiciary. Pass the Bar exam → Advocate → Experienced Advocate → State Advocate (appointed by the Interior Minister) or Magistrate (Take the Bench). Advocates take cases on contract; magistrates preside. Bench tiers replace lower titles in the topbar pill.
  • The civil service. Sit the civil-service exam to be assigned to a ministry (Defense, Foreign Affairs & Trade, Economic Development, Interior). Civil servants file paperwork and memos for the state in exchange for steady Reputation and Capital, and serve as the institutional memory of their ministry.

How a tick plays

Every tick you pick one repeatable action from your current role's card — a Community Organizer might Door-Knock, give a Speech, or Build the Base; a Civil Servant files Paperwork or a Memo; an Advocate takes or refuses a drawn case. Each action is a dice roll modified by a stat, with bracketed outcomes printed on the card so you can plan a turn before you click.

Joining a party unlocks a second class of actions — campaign work, fundraising, lobbying ministers — and gives you a stage to compete for office on. You don't have to join one to play, but the parliamentary and ministerial rungs require it.

National politics, local politics, and your first race

Once you've joined a party, an Election Offer opens up on your Career page. It presents two races side by side, and the choice between them is the first real strategic decision you'll make as a politician.

Local politics — the Community Board. This is a one-tick race for a Community Organizer seat in your district. The opponent is an auto-generated independent, the Experience matchup is small (you vs. roughly 2–4), and the loss case is harmless — you give up a tick and walk away. The win case is your first office: a 9-tick term running grassroots work, plus a starter Capital payout and a Reputation bump. Almost every politician should start here. It exists to teach you the system without risking anything you can't recover.

National politics — Parliament. A parliamentary race plays for the national stage. The Experience matchup is bigger (a wider opponent range and a higher die), the stake on the win side is the largest single payout a low-tier politician can take, and the stake on the loss side hurts — reputation, influence, capital, all real. You also wait several ticks for the result instead of one. This is the race you take after you've banked some Experience at the local level, or that you take cold if you're feeling brave.

How a race actually resolves. Experience drives both races: your Experience stat is rolled against the opponent's, the matchup converts to a polling percentage that gets snapshotted on the active-race card, and the result lands at the resolve tick. You can spend the ticks in between running campaign actions to nudge the polling — ads, rallies, door-knocking — or sitting tight and trusting the matchup. Win and the system stamps your office, pays out the rewards, and adds the entry to your career log. Lose and the loss is logged just as visibly: a defeat sits in your career history alongside your wins, and people remember.

Recommended first arc. Join a party. Run for the Community Organizer seat. Serve the 9-tick term using the Tier-1 action card (Door Knock, Speech, Build the Base). When the term ends, decide whether you want to climb laterally to City Council Member — another local race, slightly more contested — or jump straight to a Parliament run on a strong Experience stat. By the time that decision lands, you'll have a feel for whether your home is in the local trenches or the national chamber.

IV.Local Politics — the city ladder

Every nation is made of cities, and every city is a small game of its own. The local ladder — Community Organizer → City Council Member → City Council President → Mayor — is where politicians learn the trade with real stakes that stay survivable, and where the work visibly changes a place: the stats on a city's page move because someone on its council moved them.

How a city is described

Each city carries a dollar Budget plus eight stats scored 1–10 and displayed as five descriptive bands: Infrastructure, Pollution, Crime, Appeal, Growth, Unemployment, Services, Affordability. Low Unemployment is good for the city — but idle labor is cheap labor, so construction in a high-Unemployment city is discounted 3% per point. That tension is the heart of the mayor's game: a thriving town is an expensive place to build, and corporations shop around.

Tier 1 — Community Organizer

Your first office: a one-tick race against an auto-generated independent, near-unloseable for any party-backed player, and a 9-tick term. The three repeatable actions (one per tick) are Fundraising Call (1d10 + Experience; lands party funds, doubles on a natural 10, a natural 1 costs Experience), Civic Meeting (1d6 + Experience; recruits Volunteers, +2 with a popularity bump on a 6), and Office Hours ($5K of party funds for steady Party Popularity with Reputation on the bracket ends). Nine ticks after your win, the City Council window opens.

Tier 2 — City Council Member

Council races are per city — you pick which one to fight for. Seated, you work three levers: Hold City Meeting (+0.5 Influence, plus +0.1 Party Popularity per Volunteer you bring), Fundraiser (1d6 × $1K to the party war chest, +1 Volunteer on a 5–6), and Propose Ordinance — your seat at the lawmaking table.

Tier 3 — City Council President

The chair. City Hearing (+0.5 Reputation, +0.1 Party Popularity per Volunteer), Build the Base (1d6: always +1 Volunteer; +0.5 Reputation on 1–2, +1 on 3–6 — the most reliable Volunteer printer in the game), and Propose Ordinance at a −1 Volunteer cost. The CCP also breaks tied council votes — holding the chair means your ordinances rarely die on a deadlock.

Ordinances — how cities legislate

Ordinances live in a global player-authored catalog: any Mayor, Council Member, or Council President can write one (a name, a description, a cost against the city budget, and stat effects of up to ±10 across the city sheet). Proposing one in your city opens a 3-tick council vote — every council seat votes, simple majority, CCP breaks ties. Pass it and the effects land on the city's stats; an active ordinance can later be rescinded by the same process. This is the cleanest way to move Crime, Pollution, or Unemployment numbers you can point at on the city page.

Tier 4 — Mayor

Mayor races are per city too (the capital has its own contest), and the term runs 12 ticks — with re-election at the end. Lose that re-election and you are evicted on the spot; an NPC takes the office. The mayor's levers run the city itself:

  • Collect Taxes — +$⌊Growth / 3⌋ to the city budget, at −1 Party Popularity. Citizens notice.
  • Campaign — +1 Influence, +1 Reputation for you, −1 Growth for the city. The classic incumbent's trade.
  • Tax Package — Minor / Moderate / Major. Pay Growth upfront and take an Unemployment hit; in exchange the next 2–5 construction requests in your city get 10% off, and each one that lands rebates +1 Growth and −1 Unemployment. Spend now to attract builders, recover as the cranes go up.

Strategy

Volunteers are the local engine. Meetings and Hearings pay Party Popularity per Volunteer, so the loop is: print Volunteers cheap (Civic Meeting early, the CCP's Build the Base later), then spend the crowd on popularity actions. Party Popularity in turn drives your starting odds in every election — local work literally makes your next race easier.

The city is a shared board with the entrepreneurs. A corporation's plant expansion in your city brings +2 Growth and −3 Unemployment on completion — the single biggest stat swing available — and businesses choose cities by construction price. A mayor running tax packages in a high-Unemployment town is setting the table for exactly those deals; befriend your entrepreneur players, or play one yourself.

Know which chair you want. The CCP chair is the legislature play — tie-breaks plus the Volunteer printer. The mayoralty is the executive play — budget, packages, and your name on the city. Both feed a parliamentary career: the popularity and Reputation banked locally carry straight into the national odds formula. And if national politics chews you up, the city ladder is always there to rebuild from.

V.National Politics — the chamber

National politics is the legislature: the National Assembly and the chairs that run the chamber itself. Where the city ladder is retail — volunteers and storefronts — the chamber is institutional. Laws are written here, taxes are set here, and the careers are longer because the terms are longer: a parliamentary cycle runs ~48 ticks in most nations, with every seat reseating at the general election.

Tier 1 — Member of Parliament

Term: until the nation's next general election (the full ~48-tick cycle if you win early in one). The race: an Experience matchup against a generated opponent or another player, entered through your party for a $50K party-funds filing fee — your party banks the seat when you win. A re-election window opens 5 ticks before the general election; file or your term simply ends, and since the chamber's reform, losing a re-election evicts you on the spot — the seat and your party's count go with it.

Actions (one per tick): Propose New Statute — draft articles into the Criminal, Civil, Commercial, or Electoral code and put the bill straight to the assembly; Propose New Law — move to flip an existing national policy to a different option, voted on the same way; Fundraising — 1d20 for thousands in donations at −0.5 party popularity; Propose Tax Holiday — file a one-time corporate tax break (years, sectors, percentage) for the chamber to decide.

Tier 2 — Senior MP

Eligibility: serve a full parliamentary term as an MP first — the nomination opens only after your seat has aged the term length. The race: a contested nomination against an opponent, same Experience-driven engine. The title marks you as a second-term name the press knows; it shares the MP's action card, and it's the springboard the chamber offices look for.

The Assembly Floor

Every proposal — statute, amendment, policy switch, tax holiday — goes straight to the floor the moment it files. The division opens as a public 3-tick chamber vote in the nation's Voting section: party blocs vote by the bill's declared supporter/opposer archetypes, the sponsor's own party always backs the bill, and unaligned parties break for or against on the day. Policy switches divide by what the option actually does — a welfare expansion pulls the left aboard and pushes the libertarians off. When the window closes, a passed statute enters its Code, a passed amendment rewrites its statute's record, and a passed policy change takes effect immediately.

Deputy Speaker & Speaker of the Assembly

Gates: a sitting MP with 35 Reputation — the Deputy Speaker's party must hold the chamber plurality; the Speaker's party must hold the Head of Government. One of each per nation, held until the next general election or resignation. These are the institutional summit of the elected chamber: procedural power, not partisan power — the title amends your card and your standing rather than handing you a new action kit.

Above the chamber

The ministerial canopy — Junior Minister, Deputy Minister, Minister, Prime Minister — is appointed, not elected, and climbs through the Government Service ladder (civil-service exam upward). The chamber feeds it: appointed posts demand membership in a governing-coalition party, and if the government falls at an election, the portfolios fall with it.

Strategy

The floor is the only gate. There is no committee to bury your bill — everything you file gets its 3-tick division in public. That cuts both ways: nothing dies quietly, but nothing hides either. Count the chamber before you draft.

Pick your stakeholders like a whip-count. A bill's declared supporter and opposer archetypes become party blocs on the floor. Before drafting, look at which archetypes actually hold seats in your chamber — tag supporters who own seats, opposers who don't, and remember your own party's seats are guaranteed YES on anything you sponsor.

Respect the calendar. Everything keys off the general election: re-election filing opens 5 ticks out, Senior MP eligibility needs a full aged term, the Speakerships reset on election night, and a lost re-election now removes you from the chamber entirely. The ticks just before an election are the most consequential of the cycle — bank your Experience and party popularity before they arrive, because the odds formula reads both.

Mind the war chest. Races cost party funds to enter and Fundraising refills them at a popularity price. A party that's broke can't field you — and one that's unpopular gives you bad starting odds. The MP who alternates donor work with visible lawmaking keeps both numbers race-ready.

VI.The Businessman path

The businessman role is about building businesses inside the same political economy your politician characters are arguing over. There are two ways to play it: own the company — found a corporation, choose an industry, raise capital, make it outlast the policy environment — or work for one: answer another player's job posting, get hired into a real desk (the automotive industry runs three career tracks — Manufacturing, Commercial, and Product — each six rungs deep, from line engineer to Chief), and run that desk's own action kit every tick. The Chiefs at rung 6 effectively co-run the corp: a Chief Manufacturing Officer can fire the factory floor on their own allowance.

The three businessmen you can be

At creation, after the name, the game asks who you are — and the answer sets your starting wealth, your age, and honestly your whole opening strategy:

  • The Wealthy Entrepreneur — $65,000,000, age 60. Skip the grind: found a serious corporation on day one, buy assets, hire other players. The catch is the clock — you start old, so the empire has to come together fast. The founder's archetype.
  • The Seasoned Executive — $2,000,000, age 50. Enough to found something modest or to buy into the game gradually; experienced enough to be taken seriously. The flexible middle path — many executives work a Chief's desk at someone else's corp while their own venture incubates.
  • The Youthful Salaryman — $150,000, age 25. No empire, all runway. You can't found anything serious yet — so you get hired, climb a career track, draw a salary, and learn the industry from inside before striking out. The longest game and the purest rags-to-riches arc.

Stats

Businessmen share the same four-stat vocabulary politicians use — Reputation, Experience, Influence, Capital — but the meaning is leaned toward commerce. Reputation is how counterparties read you; Experience drives execution rolls on operations and deals; Influence opens doors with ministries and regulators; Capital is straightforwardly your money on hand.

Founding a corporation

You begin by founding a corp in one of the chartered industries:

  • Banking
  • Shipping & maritime logistics
  • Construction & engineering
  • Real estate & development
  • Civil aviation (airlines)
  • Aviation manufacturing
  • Oil & gas

Founding takes a chunk of starting capital (between $5M and $500M) plus a fixed founding fee. The corp HQs in a specific nation and lives or dies under that nation's tax code, tariff regime, and policy environment — the same one Parliament is voting on tickly. You can run the corp private (you own 100%) or take it public via an IPO to raise capital from other players, at the cost of sharing the upside.

How a tick plays

An owner's tick is dominated by the state of the corp and one executive action — every industry has a five-card desk (draft a blueprint, run production, bid on work, analyze a market, upgrade an asset) and you fire one card per tick. Employees run their own desk kits on a separate allowance. Beyond the desks you manage buildings, accept or reject contracts, file taxes, and respond to RFPs from other corps and the state. Each industry has its own operational surface.

Where it meets the rest of the game

Businessmen aren't an island — their world is the same world the politicians are governing:

  • Politicians write the rules entrepreneurs operate under (tax brackets, tariffs, employment law, subsidies). A bad Parliament can crush a healthy corp.
  • Courts settle disputes. When two corps clash, the case goes through the judicial system the Advocate / Magistrate politicians staff.
  • Ministries award contracts. A Civil Servant inside Economic Development is the person on the other side of a corp's RFP submission.

The economy page (The Sectors of the Economy) shows every industry's strength in each nation and which corporations are operating in it — that's the room your businessman is competing inside.

VII.Construction Corporations

Construction is the industry every other industry calls when it wants to exist somewhere: city governments request buildings, banks and automakers commission expansions, and your firm turns capital, materials, and time into structures with your name on the permit. It's the most logistics-flavored corp in the game — you win work by bidding, then actually have to deliver it.

The five executive actions

One per tick, from the corp page:

  • Draft Blueprint — design a structural template: building type, materials bill, build time. Blueprints are your product catalog; everything you bid or self-build comes off one.
  • Submit Public Bid — the heart of the business. Cities and corps post construction requests with escrowed budgets; you bid against rival firms, and an award turns the escrow into your contract price. Bids on requests outside your home nation gate on the top regulatory tier.
  • Advance Build — $10K per active site to push every build one tick forward. Time is money; this is buying time.
  • Procurement Run — buy Construction Materials and Heavy Equipment. What you may buy, store, and import is set by your yard tier (below).
  • Logistical Overhaul — permanently upgrade one of the five asset ladders.

The five asset ladders

Construction's depth lives in five tiered assets, upgraded through Logistical Overhaul (tiers price $7M → $10M → $16M → $25M → $40M up the rungs):

  • The Yard (Heavy Equipment) — storage and purchasing rights. Tier 0 is a rental yard that can only buy materials straight into an active project; climbing it unlocks stockpiles, foreign sourcing, and owned equipment. A build cannot complete until its blueprint's materials and equipment are supplied — the yard is why deadlines are real.
  • Project Management — how many sites you can run at once: 1 at Tier 0 scaling to unlimited at Tier V, with higher tiers gated on owning commercial buildings (in multiple nations at the top).
  • Materials — the supply-chain ladder feeding your stockpiles.
  • Regulatory Compliance — the license ladder: what you may legally build. City permits cover residential and basic work; climbing unlocks heavier infrastructure, industrial plants, defense work at Tier IV, and the Tier V International Charter — the sole gate on foreign construction.
  • System Design — the experience engine. Every finished project pays the corp Experience (more per tier), and the design tier sets the corp's Experience cap (30 → 50 → 80). Corp Experience is the firm's institutional skill — the long game.

Mechanics — how a project actually lands

Win a bid (or start a self-build on land you control — same pipeline, no client). The site appears under Active Projects with a completion date, a materials requirement, and an equipment requirement. Ticks pass; you supply materials from the yard or buy them in; Advance Build compresses the schedule when the contract is worth it. When the clock and the supplies are both satisfied, the build completes: the client's escrow pays out, the corp banks Experience through the System Design ladder, and city builds land their stat effects on the city itself. A site that hits its date without supplies simply parks — Awaiting supplies — until you deliver.

Career tracks

Hired employee tracks (the job-posting careers the automotive industry runs) haven't opened for construction yet — today the firm itself is the career. The owner climbs five ladders instead of one, and the corp's Experience pool is the résumé. When construction tracks open, expect the same shape as automotive: site and commercial tracks, six rungs, a Chief at the top with their own desk.

Strategy

Watch the cities, not just the requests. Construction is priced off city Unemployment (−3% per point) — idle towns are cheap to build in, and mayors running Tax Packages are literally subsidizing your next bid (−10% plus a stat rebate per request). The firms that read city politics underbid the ones that don't.

The yard wins deadlines. Most failed projects die at Awaiting supplies, not at the bid table. Materials stockpiled before the award beat materials scrambled after it.

Sequence the ladders. PM first (more concurrent sites = more revenue), yard second (so those sites finish), regulatory when a category you can't legally touch starts appearing in requests, design whenever the Experience cap starts pinching. And keep a politician friend in the chamber — the rules of your industry are written in a room you can't enter.
